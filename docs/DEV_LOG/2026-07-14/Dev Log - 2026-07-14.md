Narrative Log

Dev Log - 2026-07-14

This was a security proof day, and it mattered because the repo needed more than a claim that the credential history had been cleaned up. It needed evidence that the cleanup held up across the surrounding surfaces that usually get overlooked until later: reachable blobs, commit and tag messages, LFS pointers, and embedded signatures.

The first pass was the bluntest and the most necessary. The exhaustive secret scan confirmed that the rewrite removed the historical rotated credential values from the reachable content and message surfaces in the disposable mirror. That did not make the job “done”; it made it precise. The difference is important. A removal claim without a scan is just confidence with better punctuation.

From there the work moved into LFS integrity. The pointer audit showed that the rewrite preserved the LFS pointer surface exactly: no malformed pointers, no changed declared sizes, no changed object IDs, and no path drift. That is the kind of result that quietly matters because it proves the cleanup did not trade one kind of damage for another. The history is now cleaner without becoming structurally sloppy.

The last layer was the one that makes the day feel real instead of merely tidy. The signature-impact report showed that 725 signed commits lost their embedded signature material as a consequence of the rewrite. That is not a defect in the proof. It is the proof. The report does the harder thing and names the cost instead of hiding it. At that point the question stops being whether the rewrite happened and becomes whether the resulting graph should be published as-is, remediated further, or retained as a proof artifact.

So the deeper change today was not just in the history itself. It was in the repo’s ability to describe that history honestly. The system now knows the rewrite removed the secret, preserved the LFS pointers, and destroyed signature material on a large signed slice. That is enough structure to make the next decision a real one instead of a vague one.

What this makes possible next is simple: a publication decision that can be taken with eyes open.
